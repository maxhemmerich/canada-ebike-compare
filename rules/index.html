What e-bike classes mean in Canada, and where the provinces differ

Before the price, the motor or the battery: is the machine legal where you ride it? Canadian e-bike law is written in watts and kilometres per hour, province by province - not as Class 1, 2 or 3. This page sets out what the three classes are (in the words of a federal agency that uses them), what Ottawa repealed and when, where a provincial law really does define classes, and what each province requires: motor limit, assist speed, minimum age, helmet and paperwork. Every figure is quoted from a named government page and dated; where a province has not published a rule, the page says so.

The three classes, in a government's own words

Class 1, 2 and 3 are not a Canadian statute. They are the labelling convention manufacturers print on a spec sheet and federal land managers use to decide which machines may use a trail - the two sources below are where they are written down. What actually binds you on a public road is provincial, and no province writes its rules as a class number.

The three classes, as published by the sources at the foot of this page.

Class 1Pedal-assist only: the motor helps while you pedal, and stops when you stop.32 km/hNo throttleThis is the class Parks Canada permits on its designated trails and beaches. Its own rule: only pedal-assist Class 1 e-bikes are allowed on the multi-use pathway in Pacific Rim National Park Reserve, and Class 2 or 3 machines are not.
Class 2Pedal-assist or a throttle: the motor can drive the bike with no pedalling.32 km/hThrottleThe same ceiling as Class 1; the throttle is the whole difference. BC's own trails policy goes further than Parks Canada and treats a Class 2 machine as a motor vehicle as well as a cycle.
Class 3Pedal-assist only, no throttle, with a higher ceiling.45 km/hNo throttle45 km/h is above every assist ceiling written into provincial law in Canada (32 km/h, or 25 km/h for a light e-bike in British Columbia), so a Class 3 bike is a bicycle on a public road only where it is ridden to the provincial limit.

Read the table with the two sources in mind. Parks Canada publishes all three classes and permits only Class 1 on its designated trails and beaches. BC's Recreation Sites and Trails policy uses the same three and adds a sharper line: a Class 1 e-bike meets the province's definition of a motor assisted cycle, while a Class 2 or Class 3 machine is also a motor vehicle.

Ottawa: the 500 W / 32 km/h definition was repealed

The figure every shop quotes - a 500 W motor helping up to 32 km/h - was a federal definition, and it is gone. Transport Canada's own page says so: “As of Feb. 4th 2021, the definition of power assisted bicycle formerly located in subsection 2(1) of the Motor Vehicle Safety Regulations is no longer in force, hence it is no longer a benchmark for assessing compliance at manufacturing or importation of e-bicycles.”

What replaced it is an import test, not a road rule: Transport Canada now assesses a machine's on-road or off-road design first, and an e-bicycle “designed to operate at speeds no greater than 32 km/h (or 20 mph) will be considered non-regulated at importation while those that can operate at speeds greater than 32 km/h (or 20 mph) will be subject to import compliance requirements as restricted-use vehicles.”

So the honest statement of Canadian e-bike law is two-layered. Federally there is no 500 W ceiling and no 32 km/h ceiling on a public road any more - only a border test. The provinces kept the repealed wording and wrote it into their own law, which is why the same numbers appear province to province and why the province you ride in is the only rule that decides.

Where Canadian law does draw classes: British Columbia

British Columbia replaced its e-bike regulation in 2024 and is the one province that now defines classes of e-bike in law - under different names and different numbers from the three-class convention above. Its Motor Assisted Cycle (E-Bike) Regulation establishes two classes, standard and light, and the province's own page publishes both side by side.

A light e-bike is the one machinery difference that matters: its motor may not propel the cycle unless the rider is pedalling, it may not exceed 250 W, it may not assist above 25 km/h, and it may not be equipped with a throttle at all. Everything else that meets the province's criteria is a standard e-bike. Neither class needs a driver's licence, registration or insurance, and a helmet is required on both.

Ontario: two classes proposed, not in force

Ontario is the other province moving this way, and it is worth being precise about how far it has got. The Ministry of Transportation posted a proposal on the Environmental Registry (notice 026-0422, posted 23 April 2026, comment period closed 7 June 2026) to update the definition of a power-assisted bicycle and to “establish distinct classes of e-bikes with tailored operator, vehicle and safety requirements”. That is a proposal: as of the date read here, Ontario still has one category and one set of rules, and this page will not print class figures for a regulation that does not exist.

Read the motor column as the one hard line. Every province here caps a power-assisted bicycle at a 500 W continuous rating, and every one stops motor assistance at a published speed - so a motor rated above 500 W, or assistance that continues past the cut-off, is what turns a bicycle into a motor vehicle that needs a licence and insurance. Two provinces leave a rider rule open: Alberta sets a minimum age without a licence requirement, and New Brunswick's registration page sets no minimum age at all. Where a rule is not published, this page says so instead of filling the gap.

Not on this page: Nova Scotia, Prince Edward Island, Newfoundland and Labrador, Yukon, the Northwest Territories and Nunavut. This page only states what it read on a government page, and those jurisdictions' own pages have not been read yet - so they are named here rather than guessed at.

Two things follow from the table. A class number is a maker's label, not a permission: the province you ride in decides whether the machine on it is a bicycle or a motor vehicle, and it decides that in watts and km/h. And where a maker's own wording leaves the ceiling adjustable, the provincial cut-off is the number that binds - a bike set above the provincial assist speed is outside the rule for the road it is on, whatever the spec sheet calls it.
